# The Tidal Interval — Voices

## Narrative voice

The case is told in third-person past tense, in the manner of an incident report compiled after the body had been recovered and the storm had blown itself out. The detective is the camera; the world acts upon her, and she acts upon the world, and the prose records both without commentary. There are no thoughts, no feelings, no editorial — only what was seen, what was heard, what was touched, and what could be inferred from the surfaces of the island. Sentences are short. Verbs are concrete. A sensory anchor — wind in the wires, salt on the door handles, the diesel cough of the generator, the static under every radio voice — grounds every scene before any human enters it.

The setting is the Tern Island Marine Research Station on the eastern edge of a small fishing island, in the middle of the October storm season. The station has eleven permanent staff, one diesel generator that fails in heavy weather, and a satellite uplink that works reliably except when it matters. The hour is whichever hour the storm and the tide say it is; ordinary clocks lose authority here. The voice trusts the smallness of the place — eleven people, two days of weather, one path along an unfenced cliff — to make every observed detail load-bearing. Speech, when it appears at all, arrives reported and clipped: *he said the generator had cut twice in the afternoon, and that was all he said*. NPCs are never editorialised; their composure or unease is shown by what their hands did, what their eyes failed to do, where their oilskin was wet and where it was dry. Researchers are differentiated from the boat skipper differentiated from the visiting inspector by cadence, vocabulary, and the particular way each one talks about the sea.

## Suspects

### Piers Dunne, station logistics coordinator

He spoke in the careful, level cadence of a man who had spent eight years explaining schedules to people who did not want to hear them, and he placed each sentence down with the small over-clarity of someone who had nothing, in his telling, to hide. His vocabulary was practical and slightly nautical — *the resupply window*, *the lee side*, *the run-down to the slack* — and he used it as a kind of currency that belonged equally to the station and to the harbour, which were the two halves of his life. Under pressure he did not raise his voice and did not look away; he placed his hands flat on the table and let them stay there, and his answers came at the same measured pace regardless of the question. His alibi was the resupply manifest he had been working on in the common room, witnessed by the cook and the radio operator at the relevant hour; what he was hiding was the matter of the path-marker socket, the twelve degrees, the two hours before Sarah's walk, and the loan papers in his desk drawer. *He read the manifest back in his own hand and his hands did not move from the table while he read it, and the dry side of his oilskin caught the lamp as he spoke.*

### Dr Marcus Webb, visiting methodology reviewer

He spoke in the brisk, faintly impatient register of a senior academic who had spent the last week saying things people had not wanted to hear and had reached the part of the visit where he no longer cared whether they liked it. His sentences were grammatically complete and statistically literate — *the variable selection*, *the confidence band*, *the absent diagnostic* — and he produced them with the small over-precision of a man who had aired the same complaint twice already in print. Under pressure he did not soften; he tightened around the consonants and the journal letter in his briefcase was produced before any question about it had actually been put. His alibi was the dorm room he had been assigned and the absence of anyone who could verify he had stayed in it; what he was hiding was nothing — he had argued with Sarah, he had not killed her, and the case's mechanism layer is closed against him. *He spoke the methodology critique in the same paragraphs he had spoken it on Friday evening, and after the third question about Sunday his eyes went to the dorm window and stayed there.*

### Oren Halloway, station director

He spoke in the slow, administrative cadence of a man whose authority on the island rested on the orderly approval of paperwork, and whose paperwork that week had concluded with the submission of a report that would end the island as an economic place. His vocabulary was procedural and slightly defensive — *the chain of approval*, *the relevant ministry*, *the proper channels* — and he placed each word with the weight of a man who had read it back to himself before saying it. Under pressure he did not pale; he produced documents. His alibi was the Sunday evening director's log and the dinner he had taken with the cook between six and eight; what he was hiding was the gap between his stated support for Sarah's conclusions and the private memorandum to the funding council that argued against external submission. *He gave the timeline in chronological order and held the cover sheet of the Sunday log open at the relevant page for slightly longer than the question required.*

### Mira Callan, boat skipper and island liaison

She spoke in the short, weather-shaped sentences of someone who had taken the station's boat between the island and the mainland twice a week for nine years and had stopped expecting any of those crossings to be conversational. Her vocabulary was small, exact, and entirely nautical — *the run*, *the bar*, *the slack*, *the lee* — and she said almost nothing that was not load-bearing. Under pressure she did not move; her hands stayed where they had been and her eyes did not leave the detective's. Her alibi was the jetty and the boat itself, secured against the weather, witnessed by the lighthouse keeper at the relevant hour; what she was hiding was that her brother crewed on a Dunne family vessel, that she had told Piers what Sarah's report would mean, and that she had not understood, when she told him, what he would do with the knowledge. *She gave the tide times for the day in the order they had occurred, and when asked about her brother she said his name once and did not say anything else.*

### Tovi Ansgar, junior researcher

She spoke in the soft, slightly hesitant cadence of a postdoctoral researcher who had shared a dormitory wall with the victim for fourteen months and had not yet found a way to talk about her in the past tense. Her vocabulary was technical when the questions allowed it — *the survey transects*, *the variance components*, *the protected-area boundary* — and it failed her when the questions turned personal. Under pressure she did not raise her voice; she lowered it, and her hands moved, repeatedly, to the small woven bracelet on her left wrist that had been Sarah's. Her alibi was the lab and the survey notebook open at the relevant page, witnessed by no one; what she was hiding was the disagreement she had had with Sarah three weeks earlier over a single variable in the survey methodology — an argument that had been resolved, that she now wished she had not had, and that she feared would make her look like Webb. *She named the disagreement plainly and added, after a pause, that they had resolved it on the Thursday before, and her hand went to the bracelet as she said the day.*

## Witnesses

### Kestrel Lin, radio operator

She spoke in the clipped, efficient register of a woman whose working language was call-signs and acknowledgements and who had not had a reliable signal in two days. Her sentences were short and time-stamped — *fourteen-twenty, no contact*, *sixteen-oh-six, weather only*, *nineteen-hundred, lost the satellite* — and her usefulness was in the precision of those stamps. She had no tell of guilt because she had nothing to hide; her tell, when it came, was that her hand went to the silent handset on the bench whenever the hour of Sarah's walk was named. *She read the radio log entries off the page in the order they had been logged, and the dead handset on the bench did not move under her hand while she read them.*

### Niall Brackett, generator engineer

He spoke in the slow, mechanical cadence of a man who had spent the storm season in the generator room with the door shut and had not yet adjusted to being addressed at length. His vocabulary was practical and grease-stained — *the relay*, *the fuel line*, *the cut-out at twelve percent load* — and he answered each question only as far as it had been asked. Under what pressure there was, he did not flinch; he was the man who kept the lights on, and the storm had given him a great deal to do that weekend. His alibi was the generator room itself and the maintenance log entries time-stamped at quarter-hour intervals through the Sunday evening; what he had was a small useful detail — that Piers had been in and out of the common room on Sunday afternoon, and that one of those absences had been longer than the others. *He named the cut-out times and added, without being asked, that he had heard the eastern door go twice between three and five, and he said it the same way he named the cut-out times.*

### Eira Voss, cook

She spoke in the warm, slightly tired voice of a woman who fed eleven people three meals a day in a kitchen the size of a cabin and had not had a full conversation with any of them in several days. Her sentences were short and observational — what she had cooked, who had eaten, who had not come down — and she gave her account without elaboration. Her stance was unaware at first of why her observations mattered, and shifted to cooperative once she understood. Her tell was that her hands kept moving across the apron at her waist whether she was speaking or listening. *She said Sarah had taken her tea at six and had said she would walk at seven as she always did, and Eira's hand moved across the apron as she said the hour.*

### Padraic Burne, lighthouse keeper

He spoke in the spare, weather-shaped cadence of a man who lived alone on a headland half a mile from the station and who valued each sentence on the principle that nobody else was likely to say it for him. His vocabulary was nautical and elemental — *the bar*, *the swell*, *the wind backing southerly* — and he used it without softening. He had no tell at all; he had been on the headland through the relevant hour and had seen what he had seen, which was a single lantern on the eastern path at quarter past seven and the same lantern not visible at quarter to eight. *He gave the two times and did not embellish either of them, and when asked whether he had seen anyone else on the path he said he had not, and that was that.*
