# Resonance Test — Voices

## Narrative voice

The case is told in third-person past tense, in the manner of a recovered conservatory record. The detective is the camera; the building acts upon her — its cold rooms, its tape hiss, its uneven stairs — and she acts upon it, and the prose registers both without commentary. There are no thoughts, no feelings, no wonderings. Only what was seen, what was heard, what was sounded, and what was inferred from those things in the flat, observational cadence of an Obra Dinn cartouche. Sentences are short. Verbs are concrete. A sensory anchor — the radiator's tick, the smell of rosin, the green light of a reel-to-reel meter — grounds every scene before any human enters it.

The setting is the Aldgate Conservatory, a Victorian warehouse on a narrow East London street, converted to teaching use in 1962 and never properly heated since. The hour is the winter term of 1974; the lamps are sodium where they are new and gas where they have not yet been changed; the radiators tick and stop and tick again. Speech, when it appears, arrives reported and clipped — *she said she had been home by nine-thirty, and the neighbour bore that out, and after that she did not enlarge upon the point*. NPCs are never editorialised; their disquiet is shown by what their hands did, where their eyes failed to go, which page of the score they would not turn. The voice trusts the reader to assemble the case from observed surfaces — and from heard ones, since this case is, more than anything, a case about listening.

## Suspects

### Marta Solís — junior tutor in harmony and counterpoint

She spoke softly, in the precise, unhurried English of a woman who had been speaking it carefully for twenty years and had not yet allowed it to soften. Her sentences were short, complete, and finished cleanly; she did not over-explain. Under pressure she became, if anything, more measured — the diction tightened by a degree, the pauses lengthened, the eyes settled on the middle distance and stayed there. Her hands stayed in her lap. There was no tremor in them. *She gave the detective her address and the time of her return — nine-thirty — and the name of the neighbour who had seen her, and after that she did not speak unless spoken to.* Her alibi was the neighbour and the hour; what she was hiding was the manuscript, the disappearance, the twenty years of another name. She had not touched Ashby. She did not need to. She knew the building.

### James Okafor — third-year composition student

He spoke quickly and at length, in the careful, slightly over-formal English of a young man whose scholarship depended on being articulate. His sentences began with concessive clauses — *granted that I argued with him, granted that I had reason to —* and his hands moved as he spoke, finding the edge of a desk, the strap of a satchel, the cuff of a sleeve. Under pressure he over-explained, producing a verbatim account of Wednesday's lunch argument that he had clearly run through in his head a hundred times since the body was found. The tell was the running through. *He told the detective what he had said to Ashby and what Ashby had said to him and what he had said afterwards in his own room, and he told it twice, without prompting, and the second telling was shorter than the first by precisely the apology he had wished he had made.* His alibi was the practice room he had been sitting in alone, which was no alibi at all, and what he was hiding was nothing except his fear that none of this would be heard.

### Peter Haldane — conservatory caretaker

He spoke in the slow, even cadence of a man who had worked in the building for sixteen years and had learnt to apologise for it. His sentences were short and freighted with qualifiers — *as best I remember, what I would say is, by my own reckoning* — and his vocabulary was small and worn. He kept his cap on his knee while he spoke. Under pressure he did not pale or stiffen; he went on, slowly, in the same tone, simply offering more of the same uncertain answer until the question went away. *He said he had written the handrail up three times, and he said he had walked past it more times than that, and he said the order to mend it had not come down, and he said it all without raising his voice and without looking up from the cap.* His alibi was the porter's lodge and a kettle, and what he was hiding was the thing he was not hiding at all — a quiet sixteen-year shame at having walked past the rail and not bound it himself.

### Nikolai Renko — visiting examiner, panel composer

He spoke in the trim, slightly accented English of a man who had emigrated from somewhere middle-European in the early fifties and had used the language with care ever since. His sentences were short, precise, and a degree colder than the room. He did not embellish; he did not soften; he answered the question that had been asked and did not answer the question that had not. Under pressure he closed; he had a stillness that was not the stillness of fear. The tell was that the stillness extended into the hands. *He said he had known of Ashby's Vienna quartet since 1956 and had played it once, on a small Berlin programme, and had thought even then that it was not the work of the man whose name was on it, and he said so without varying his expression or his pitch.* His alibi was a hotel ledger in Bayswater and what he was hiding, faintly, was that he had begun making his own inquiries about the quartet two years ago and had not yet finished them.

## Witnesses

### Professor Eleanor Fairchild — chair of the examining panel

She spoke in the careful, institutional English of someone who had spent thirty years writing letters that committed the conservatory to nothing. Her sentences were complete, polite, and faintly recursive — *to the extent that I am in a position to say, and with appropriate caution as to what may yet emerge —* and her vocabulary belonged to committee minutes more than to music. She was cooperative on the surface and immoveable below it. The tell was the recursion. *She gave the detective an account of Monday's session and Tuesday's session and Wednesday's session, in that order, and she gave the times correctly and the participants correctly and the conclusions only insofar as the panel had reached them, and the conclusions the panel had not yet reached she did not give.* Her alibi was the panel meeting in the faculty lounge, and what she was hiding was the long, slow institutional habit of letting men like Ashby alone.

### Mr Norwood — archive clerk, Royal Academy of Music

He spoke in the soft, mildly bored voice of a man who had worked alone among paper for a long time and was glad of company without quite knowing how to use it. His sentences were short and accurate; he gave dates without consulting and shelf numbers without checking. He had a precise memory and no investment in what it produced. He was the case's quiet pivot — he held, in a single drawer, the manuscript that would unmake Ashby's twenty years — and he did not know it. *He said the uncollected student work of 1951 to 1953 occupied three drawers of the south cabinet and that one of those drawers held a score in a small clear handwriting belonging to a name he could read aloud if the detective wished, and the detective wished, and he read it: Elena Varga.* His alibi was the archive office and a sandwich. He was hiding nothing.

### the practice-room attendant

He spoke in monosyllables and the occasional half-sentence, in the manner of a man on a small wage who answered the same three questions every evening for ten years and had stopped finding them interesting. His sentences were complete enough — *yes*, *no*, *just gone nine*, *only the one* — and his attention sat on the sign-in book in front of him. His tell was the absence of one: he had no reason to lie and produced none. *He said Professor Ashby had signed in to the basement practice rooms at quarter past nine on Thursday evening and had not signed out, and that he himself had locked the door at half ten as instructed, and that he had not gone down to check because he had not been told to.* His alibi was the sign-in book and his own seat by it. He was hiding nothing.

### Miss Pryce — ensemble leader

She spoke in the energetic, slightly performative English of a third-year string player who had been on stage three times in the previous week and had not yet come down from it. Her sentences ran on and corrected themselves cheerfully; she was eager to be useful and unsure how to be. The tell was simply her youth. *She said the quartet had been a beautiful piece, hadn't it, and very difficult in the second movement, and Professor Ashby had asked them to take the slow section once more without the cello, and that was the only odd thing she could think of, and she did not seem to find it very odd.* Her alibi was the rehearsal room and three other players, and she was hiding nothing she knew of.

### Mrs Thirsk — Marta Solís's neighbour

She spoke at length, in the rounded, cheerful English of a woman who had lived on the same East London street for forty years and considered every person who passed her window to be an item worth discussing. Her sentences were generous and circular and arrived eventually at the point. The tell was that the point, when it came, was exact: she remembered the hour because the television had been showing the close of the nine o'clock news. *She said Marta had come up the steps at quarter to ten or just after, by her reckoning, and had said good evening, and had gone in, and her own light had come on in the front room a minute later, and Mrs Thirsk had watched it come on through her own window.* Her alibi was her armchair and the television. She was hiding nothing.

### the night porter

He spoke briskly, in the clipped, slightly aggrieved English of a man who had run the lodge gate alone every winter night for eleven years and resented being interrupted in it. His sentences were short and procedural. He kept a small black notebook open on his counter while he spoke, in case the times were doubted. *He said he had locked the courtyard gate at ten o'clock, as instructed, and that Professor Ashby had been the last man inside and had not come out, and that no one else had passed him after nine, and that the lights in the practice wing had gone out from the ground-floor panel at nine fifteen — which he had noticed because it was not his hand on the switch.* His alibi was the lodge and the notebook. He was hiding nothing.

### the conservatory records clerk

He spoke in the dry, slightly tired English of a man who kept paper for a living and had grown to dislike the people who came asking for it. His sentences were short and exact. He produced documents without elaboration and put them away again the moment the detective had read enough. The tell was a small, dry impatience — he wished the matter to be done with. *He said the maintenance log carried three entries for the practice-wing handrail, in November of seventy-two and March of seventy-three and September of seventy-four, and that each entry bore the caretaker's mark and a panel stamp acknowledging receipt, and that none of them bore the second stamp indicating that a work order had been raised.* His alibi was the records office. He was hiding nothing — not even the small, unfunny fact that the conservatory had ignored its own log three times in two years.
