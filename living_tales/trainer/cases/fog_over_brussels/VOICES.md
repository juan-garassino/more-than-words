# Fog Over Brussels — Voices

## Narrative voice

The case is told in third-person past tense, in the manner of a recovered diplomatic file — the cable copy, the seating plan, the post-mortem panel, all of it written up after the fact and read now by someone who was not in the room. The detective is the camera; the embassy acts upon her, and she acts upon the embassy, and the prose records both without commentary. There are no thoughts, no feelings, no wonderings — only what was seen, what was heard, what was touched, and what was inferred from those things in the flat, methodical cadence of an Obra Dinn cartouche.

The setting is the Belgian Embassy on the Rue de la Loi, Brussels, December 4th, 1961, between the hours of eight and eleven pm. The fog outside is the colour of cold milk and presses against the windows in the east wing where the heating is working and the windows in the west wing where it is not. The reception below is sixty-three guests in four languages, and the murmur of it carries up the stairwell as a single sound. Speech, when it appears at all, arrives reported and clipped — *he said the corridor had taken him no more than the usual minute, and that was all he said*. NPCs are never editorialised; their composure is shown by what their hands did, what their eyes failed to do, what register their French was pitched in. The voice trusts the reader to assemble the case from observed surfaces, exactly as the detective must.

A sensory anchor — fog, brass, cold tile, heated air, the smell of crystal and candle smoke — grounds every scene before any human enters it.

## Suspects

### Brennan, Cultural Attache

He spoke in the cultivated, faintly Irish-inflected English of a man who had worked diplomatic postings across four countries since 1949, and his charm was the kind that had been practised for so long it could no longer be told apart from the man underneath it. His sentences were short, lightly ornamented, and finished cleanly; he spoke French at official pitch and a flatter, less courteous French in the corridors, and he switched between them without ever appearing to. Under pressure he did not over-explain. He turned the signet ring on the third finger of his right hand, once, fully, and answered the next question in a voice that was a fraction lower than the one before it. His alibi for the corridor was the usual minute, the usual corridor, the usual tray; what he was hiding was the forty seconds in which he had intercepted the tray, and the eighteen-month-old copy of Ilse's desk key, and four years of compliance that had begun in 1955 with something he had been unable to give up. *He answered the question about the corridor and turned the ring on his right hand without looking down at it, and the answer was the same length as the others and did not pause anywhere a pause was expected.*

**Voice stages**

- **cold** — sentences trimmed and pleasant; the charm is fully deployed; the signet ring is not touched; the French is correct without being notable; eye contact is given evenly. The reader should feel met by a courteous, peripheral figure.
- **warming** — answers grow a little shorter, a little more precise; the ring is touched once in passing; a brief switch into French where English would have served; the smile holds. The shape of an organised man surfacing under the charm.
- **breaking** — the ring is turned through a full revolution mid-answer; the French becomes a fraction too correct, the kind of correctness that comes from rehearsal rather than fluency; a pause lands somewhere a pause was not invited; the answer about the corridor is repeated word for word from earlier in the evening.
- **broken** — the voice drops half a tone; the ring is held rather than turned; the courtesy is replaced by a flat, considered silence between sentences; the corridor is acknowledged in a tense that the earlier accounts had not used. He does not confess. He stops covering.

### Helena Voss, Ambassador's Wife

She spoke in the cool, fluent French of a woman who had lived inside diplomatic protocol for thirty years and had stopped noticing where protocol ended and she began. Her English was crisp, careful, and deployed only when politeness made it the better choice. Her sentences were complete and finished, and she did not interrupt herself or anyone else. Under questioning her hands stayed on her champagne glass and her shoulders did not move; her tell was that her wedding band caught the light when she turned her wrist, and that she turned it more often as the questions came closer to the corridor she had been in and would not yet name. Her alibi was the forty minutes she had been away from the reception and her account of where she had been was firm in everything except the geography that bracketed the document room corridor. *She gave the time of her departure and the time of her return without consulting anyone, and her hand did not leave the stem of the glass while she did so.*

**Voice stages**

- **cold** — perfectly composed; offers the usual courtesies; the timing of her absence is stated cleanly and not elaborated; the band catches the light only at the natural turn of her wrist.
- **warming** — answers extend a fraction; a small, dry observation about the seating plan offered unprompted; the band is turned once; the protocol-French softens by a tone into something approaching candour.
- **breaking** — the geography of her absence becomes evasive in a way that her timing did not; the band is turned twice in a single answer; she repeats a phrase from her first account word for word as though to settle herself in it; her glass is set down and picked up again.
- **broken** — she names the corridor. She does not name what she was doing there. She names what she saw — the tray, the figure who took it, the forty seconds — and after she names it she does not look up. The composure does not break; it is set aside.

### The Catering Supervisor

He spoke in the brisk, plain Flemish-accented French of a man who had run thirty diplomatic receptions and could see in his head where every tray ought to be at every minute of the evening. His sentences were short, specific, and full of times — *eight-oh-six, the second tray; eight-fourteen, the kitchen-side door; eight-fifteen, the chandelier line.* He spoke them without consulting his notes because his notes were where he had put them after the fact, not how he had run the night. Under pressure he did not soften and did not stiffen; he became more exact, not less. His tell was a small pencil he carried in his apron pocket, which he produced and held but did not always use; when the question was the right question the pencil came out and the answer was a time, given in three syllables. His alibi was the kitchen door and his timing book; what he had was the forty seconds and the certainty that the tray that should have taken twenty had taken forty, and the corridor that should have held no one had held someone, and that he had thought nothing of it at the time because the reception was running and the reception had not stopped. *He named the forty-second interval and the tray it belonged to and the corridor it had crossed and he did not name the figure that had intercepted it because he had not seen the figure, and the pencil stayed in his hand without being used.*

**Voice stages**

- **cold** — answers procedural questions with the timing book closed on the counter; gives general accounts of the evening; the pencil stays in the apron.
- **warming** — the timing book is opened; specific minutes are produced; small unprompted observations about kitchen flow surface; the pencil is in his hand, point down.
- **breaking** — the forty-second interval is named, plainly, without commentary; the pencil tip rests on the page without drawing; he waits to be asked the next question rather than continuing.
- **broken** — the corridor is named, the tray is named, the time the tray left and the time the tray arrived are named, and what fits between those two times is named as a count of seconds rather than as an event. He does not name a figure. He gives the detective the window inside which the figure must be found.

## Other present persons (witnesses, support)

### The Ambassador Voss

He spoke in the measured, slightly worn French of a diplomat in his fourth decade of service and in the careful English of one who knew that English was the language his guests preferred when matters became serious. His sentences were short, declarative, and final. He answered every question put to him about the reception and refused, politely and without elaboration, every question put to him about his wife's whereabouts that night. His tell was a small one: the cuff of his shirt was a quarter-inch longer at one wrist than the other, and when he was tired the long cuff was pulled at and the short one was not. He was not implicated. He was protective. *He answered each question about the guests by name and each question about the corridor by reference to the chief of security, and the longer cuff was pulled at twice in the course of half an hour.*

### The Cipher Supervisor

He spoke in the dry, precise vocabulary of his trade — codes, panels, key length, substitution depth — and resented questions that strayed from it. He had noticed Ilse's pattern recognition twice in three years and had written it up in her file twice and had not acted on it either time, and the file in question lay on his desk in plain view as if to make the point that he had not concealed his failure to act. His sentences were short and complete. His tell was that he answered questions about the November 28th dispatch fully and questions about Ilse herself in fewer words each time. *He produced the file and named the two entries by their date and did not name the third he had thought of writing.*

### The Embassy Doctor

He spoke in the patient, clinical cadence of a man who attended diplomatic receptions because his contract required it and would have preferred to be elsewhere. His sentences were short and medical. He had recorded cardiac arrest at ten forty-seven pm because cardiac arrest was what he had seen, and he had recorded it without further annotation because in 1961 the panel he could call for did not extend to the compound in question. His tell was that he answered every clinical question without hesitation and every question about the panel itself with a slight, professional pause. *He gave the cause as cardiac arrest and the time as ten forty-seven and answered the question about the panel by naming the panel and not by defending it.*

### The Catering Runner

He spoke in plain Brussels Flemish and broken French, in the apologetic register of a man who had not been told he might be questioned and would have preferred not to be. His sentences trailed off. He had carried the tray from the kitchen and handed it on at the reception room door and could not say what had happened to it in between because he had been called back to the kitchen by the supervisor for a second load. His tell was that his hands kept moving against the front of his apron in long, unconscious passes. He was not implicated. He was the means by which the tray had crossed the corridor without being attended for forty seconds. *He said he had carried the tray and been called back and that was all he could say about it, and his hand moved against the apron as he said it.*

### The Press Attache

He spoke in the easy, mobile French of a man whose work was to move through a room and remember who had been beside whom and for how long. His sentences were long and parenthetical and full of names. He was cooperative without being implicated, and his usefulness was the seating-plan geography of the reception. *He named seven guests in the order he had seen them between eight and eight-thirty and named two of them again as having been near the kitchen-corridor door, and his French did not slip anywhere.*

### The Chief of Security

He spoke in the clipped, official English that embassy security used when speaking to outsiders, and his French when speaking to the staff. His sentences were short and squared. He produced the logs that were his to produce and declined, politely, to produce the logs that were not. His tell was the angle at which he held his folder — closed and squared against his chest when answering questions about his own watch; opened and tilted toward the detective when answering questions about staff movement. *He gave his watch in the order he had walked it and the corridor entry as having been within the usual minute, and the folder stayed squared against his chest while he gave it.*

### The Kitchen Porter

He spoke in plain Flemish and a thin, irregular French, in the cadence of a man who had been on his feet for ten hours and had another four ahead of him. His sentences were short and observational — what trays he had loaded, what the supervisor had told him, what door he had closed behind him. He was cooperative and unaware. His tell was that he kept rubbing the back of his hand against the front of his apron as he spoke. *He said he had loaded the second tray and closed the kitchen-side door behind it and had seen no one in the corridor because the corridor had not been his to watch, and his hand passed against the apron as he said it.*
