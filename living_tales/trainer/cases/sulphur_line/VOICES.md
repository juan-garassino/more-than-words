# Sulphur Line — Voices

## Narrative voice

The case is told in third-person past tense, in the manner of a recovered Board of Trade casebook. The detective is the camera; the works act upon her — the pale smoke from the third processing line, the smell of sulphur that does not leave a wool coat once it has settled into it, the gas lamp over the gate that flickers on the half-hour and steadies after — and she acts upon it, and the prose records both without commentary. There are no thoughts, no feelings, no wonderings. Only what was seen, what was weighed, what was read off a ledger entry that did not match the entry above it, and what was inferred from those things in the flat, observational cadence of an Obra Dinn cartouche. Sentences are short. Verbs are concrete. A sensory anchor — the chimney smoke settling against the winter sky, the canal water gone the colour of weak tea, the gas lamps coming on in the works yard at four — grounds every scene before any human enters it.

The setting is the Hartwick Chemical Works, on the eastern edge of an unnamed northern industrial city, February 1889. Twelve acres of brick compound, a canal along the southern edge, a chimney that releases a pale yellow smoke that drifts over the towpath and never altogether dissipates. The works produces sulphuric acid, bleaching compounds, and a proprietary textile chemical that has made its owner moderately wealthy and its workers moderately ill. Speech, when it appears, arrives reported and clipped — *he said the valve had been inspected two weeks before the failure and the entry in the maintenance log would show as much, and he said it in the same flat cadence with which he had given the times of the morning shift change*. NPCs are never editorialised; their concealment is shown by what their hands did when the ledger was set in front of them, by which entries they offered to read aloud and which they did not, by the small particulars of ink and handwriting that a twenty-year inspector's eye sees and a casual reader does not. The voice trusts the reader to assemble the case from observed surfaces, exactly as Gould was doing when the canal path took him. Above all, the voice registers what the works books said and what the works did, and the precise small differences between the two.

## Suspects

### Silas Oake — works manager, the killer

He spoke in the slow, measured cadence of a man who had managed twelve acres of chemical production for twenty-three years and had learnt to put his answers into the same shape as a maintenance log entry. His sentences were short, complete, and finished cleanly; he did not over-explain. Under pressure he became more measured rather than less — the pauses lengthened by a beat, the diction tightened by a degree, the hand that had been resting on the desk stayed there. His voice did not rise. He did not pale. The tells he produced were small and almost procedural — the half-second pause when the September 1886 entry was named, the way he offered the maintenance log open at the wrong week, the slight thickening of the sulphur smell on his coat where it had hung longer in the office than it ought to have. None of these were evidence by themselves. Together they were the portrait of a man who had spent three days knowing what was coming and had spent twenty-three years before that knowing how to keep a book in a shape that would survive any inspection but the one Gould was now giving him. *He gave the detective an account of his Thursday evening — supper at home, a walk before bed as was his habit, his wife had seen him leave the house after seven — and he gave it without consulting the duty log, and when the question came about the service road behind the sulphur storage he paused for the same half-second he had paused over the September 1886 entry, and then he said he could not say he had taken it that evening but could not say he had not.* His alibi was the house and the wife's account; what he was hiding was the walk on the towpath, the button lost at the bend, the maintenance log entries he had gone back and revised in a slightly different ink, and the three-day gap in the accident log from September 1886 that was not the gap itself but the shadow of it.

### James Whitlow — compensated worker, the red herring

He spoke in the blunt, slightly aggrieved English of a man who had worked a processing line for fifteen years and had been paid for his silence and had begun to suspect the silence was not properly purchased. His sentences were short and unembellished; he did not begin them with concessive clauses. Under pressure he did not soften and did not stiffen — he held the same flat, slightly resentful cadence he held when he was not under pressure. He had been seen near the works twice in the past month. He had spoken in a public house about injustice in general terms. He had not yet signed the document Oake's solicitor was preparing. *He told the detective he had taken the payment because there was no other payment to be had, and that he had not signed the document because the document had not yet been put to him, and that he had spoken of injustice in a public house because injustice had been done, and he said the three things in the order they had happened and gave the names where he had them.* His alibi for the Thursday evening was the public house and three men who would say so; what he was hiding was not the killing of Gould — he had not seen Gould — but the September 1886 valve failure as it had actually happened, which he had been carrying since and which, when it surfaced in his LATE phase testimony, saturated the motive dim completely and explained why Gould had to die before he filed his report rather than after.

### Foreman Craggs — foreman of the third processing line

He spoke in the careful, slightly defensive English of a man who had taken his foreman's papers at twenty-seven and had not had a quiet shift since. His sentences began with the operative qualifier — *to the best of my recollection, the entry as I made it at the time, the inspection as it was carried out on the day* — and his hands moved as he spoke, finding the corner of the maintenance log, the wire of his keys at his belt, the bracket on which the log was mounted. Under pressure he over-explained the procedural side and went quiet on the personal one. The revised entries in the log were in a hand that was not entirely his and an ink that was from a later batch, and he knew it and did not say it. *He told the detective the order of the valve inspections for the third processing line as the log recorded them and gave the dates without being asked, and when the question came about whether any entries had been revised after the fact he said the log was the log and that he had signed each entry on the day, and the silence after he said it was a degree longer than the silences between his other answers.* His alibi was the foreman's station and the men on his line; what he was hiding was that Oake had asked for the log after the failure and had returned it with entries he had not made.

### Mrs Oake — wife

She spoke in the practised, slightly distant English of a woman who had been the manager's wife for nineteen years and had learnt to keep an account of her husband's hours that would survive any neighbour's gossip. Her sentences were short and exact; she gave the hour of supper and the hour her husband left the house as though reading from a household book. Under pressure she did not soften. The tell was the exactness — she remembered the hour because she had remembered it on purpose. *She said supper had been at half past six on the Thursday and that Silas had left the house after as he did every Thursday, and that she had not looked at the clock when he returned but that the fire had been low and the supper things still on the table and she put the time at nine, and she gave the three particulars in the order in which they had occurred and offered no further detail.* Her alibi was the kitchen and the supper things; what she was hiding was that she did not know whether her husband had taken the canal path that night and did not particularly want to.

### Alderman Hicks — borough alderman

He spoke in the polished, slightly bored English of a man who held three positions of local trust and considered the works one of them. His sentences were complete, polite, and slightly recursive — *to the extent that the matter falls within the licensing committee's competence, and with appropriate caution as to what may yet be the inspector's finding —* and his vocabulary belonged to the council chamber more than to the works floor. Under pressure he became more institutional. *He said the licensing committee had deferred the previous year's inspection on a procedural matter and that the procedural matter had been raised by counsel for the works and was within the committee's discretion to accept, and he gave each clause in the order in which it appeared in the minutes.* His alibi was the council chamber on Thursday afternoon and the family rooms on Thursday evening; what he was hiding was a small, steady arrangement with the works that had been profitable to both sides.

### Dr Poole — works physician

He spoke in the dry, formal English of a country doctor who had filled out company forms for nine years and had stopped noticing what the forms did not ask. His sentences were short and clinical and tended to invoke the form rather than the case. Under pressure he became more clinical. *He told the detective that Birch had presented in 1886 with respiratory symptoms consistent with acute industrial exposure and that the cause-of-death certificate had been filled out in the standard form for natural workplace hazard and signed in the office of the works, and he gave each particular in the order required by the form.* His alibi was the surgery and the rounds book; what he was hiding was that he had not asked questions of Oake that a doctor not paid by the works might have asked.

## Witnesses

### the works clerk

She spoke in the patient, repetitive register of a woman who had been at the dispatch desk for eleven years and could give a ledger figure to the shilling without consulting the page. Her sentences were complete, polite, and slightly under-volume, as though she did not wish to disturb the office. She had no tell to speak of — her hands stayed on the ledger before her, steady — and what she had to give was simple: the sulphur arrivals as weighed had not matched the arrivals as ledgered for the last quarter, and she had been told the scales were being recalibrated, and the scales had not been recalibrated. *She said the difference had been a half-hundredweight one week and a hundredweight the next, and that she had brought it to Mr Craggs's notice and to Mr Oake's, and that she had been told both times that the scales would be looked at, and she gave the figures off the ledger without opening the ledger.*

### the night watchman

He spoke in the spare, unhurried English of a man who had walked the works boundary every night for six years and considered the silence of the gate the proper subject of his report. His sentences were short and observational — what he had seen at what time, what gate he had been at, what hour the lamp had been lit. He gave times in the order he had reached them. *He said Mr Oake had left by the main gate at quarter past six on the Thursday evening and had not come back by the gate, and that the service road behind the sulphur storage was not on his round but ran into the lane behind the works, and that he had not been on the lane at the relevant hour.*

### the canal bargee

He spoke in the rough, easy dialect of a man who had worked his horse along the cut from the wharf to the bridge for thirty years and had no patience for a question that could not be answered with a fact. His sentences were short and ran on slightly when they did. *He said he had taken the boat up at half past five on the Thursday and had seen two men walking the towpath in the direction of the works, and that he had taken the boat back at half past eight and had seen one of them returning by the service road and the other not at all, and that he had not seen the face of either at any distance he could properly call a face.*

### the widow Birch

She spoke quietly, in the soft consonants of a woman who had been a widow for two and a half years and had been told for two and a half years not to speak. Her sentences were short and complete and ended where they ended. The tell was the exactness — she remembered the sum to the shilling. *She said her husband had been brought home from the works on a stretcher on the Saturday morning and had died at the hospital on the Wednesday, and that Mr Oake had visited her on the Thursday with a document and a sum, and that she had signed the document and taken the sum and had not read the document, and she gave the sum to the shilling and the document she could not give because Mr Oake had taken the document with him.*

### the dispatch porter

He spoke in the abbreviated, slightly tired English of a man who loaded sacks from the rail siding to the shed every Tuesday and Friday and had loaded sacks for eight years and had not loaded the sacks on the Friday after the inspection had begun. His sentences were short and unembellished. *He said he had been told the Friday load was not required and that he had not been told why, and that the load had been required every Friday for eight years before, and that he had not seen the rail wagon turned back and could not say where the sacks had gone.* His alibi was the workers' lockers and the loading bench. He was hiding nothing.
