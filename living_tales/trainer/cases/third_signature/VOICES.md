# Third Signature — Voices

## Narrative voice

The case is told in third-person past tense, in the manner of a recovered casebook from the Aldgate Club's own archive. The detective is the camera; the world acts upon her, and she acts upon the world, and the prose records both without commentary. There are no thoughts, no feelings, no editorialising — only what was seen, what was heard, what was touched, and what could be inferred from the surfaces of a club evening in winter. Sentences are short. Verbs are concrete. A sensory anchor — pipe smoke held in the carved wainscot, coal-fire warmth on a polished brass fender, the cold of a fountain pen, the faint resin of old paper in the manuscript archive — grounds every scene before any human enters it.

The setting is the Aldgate Club, Bloomsbury, a Georgian townhouse converted to a literary club a century earlier and never since modernised. The hour is the slow descent of a Wednesday evening in winter, 1935, between the dinner gong and the discovery in the reading room at ten fifteen. The voice trusts the mannered restraint of inter-war London letters — the careful neutrality of literary executors, the deliberate softness of a gentleman publisher, the polished hedging of a litigation solicitor — to collide silently with the violence done in the port glass. Speech, when it appears at all, arrives reported and clipped: *he said the eight-five entry had been her own hand, and that was all he said*. NPCs are never editorialised; their composure or trembling is shown by what their hands did, what their eyes failed to do, where their signature had landed and where it had not. Members are differentiated from staff differentiated from outsiders by cadence, vocabulary, and the particular way each one falters under the same kind of pressure.

The register is between-wars London: *gentleman publisher*, *the bound proof*, *the typescript margin*, *the dedication page*, *gin-and-soda*, *the literary executor*, *the trust deed*, *the sign-in book*, *the porter's lodge*. No anachronism; no modern idiom. The detective herself is not named; she is the recording instrument.

## Suspects

### Agnes Vail (killer — top NPC, full voice arc)

She spoke in the mannered, faintly old-fashioned diction of a woman who had been a literary hostess for thirty years and had calibrated every sentence she uttered in society against the possibility that it would be remembered. Her vocabulary was old-Bloomsbury — *most regrettable*, *one is given to understand*, *the dear man* — and she used it as a velvet stop on any subject she did not wish opened. Under pressure she did not stiffen; she went *more cordial*, and the small mannerisms of the practiced dinner-guest became more visible: the slight inclination of the head, the unhurried turning of her wedding ring, the way the black gloves were not removed even by the coal fire in the smoking room. Her alibi was the sign-in book entered at eight-five and exited at eight-forty, which she invited the detective to consult as though it ended the matter. What she was hiding was that those thirty-five minutes had been long enough — that Wills had been at the door with a member's hat for seven of them, that the port decanter had been hers alone in that interval, and that her hand, which had signed the eight-five entry in the unmistakable leftward slope that had also signed the 1921 trust deed as its third signatory, would in time betray her on paper. *She removed her gloves only to write, and what she wrote in the register was the same hand that had written the letters of 1918 and the dedication of 1922 and the deed of 1921, and the detective knew it before Agnes had finished the loop of the V.*

### Carey (rival estate solicitor — false plateau / red herring — top NPC, full voice arc)

He spoke in the smooth, professionally hedged register of a litigation solicitor whose working hours were spent calibrating exactly how much to say to whom and under what understanding. His sentences were grammatically complete, legally cushioned, and arrived with the small structural tics of his trade — *insofar as my instructions allow*, *without prejudice*, *I am bound to observe*, *the Marsh matter*. Under pressure his charm did not crack; it thickened, and the briefcase he carried under one arm was repositioned a half-turn between each answer. His alibi was the dinner he had attended in the dining room from seven-thirty until shortly after nine, witnessed by two members; his location near the reading-room corridor at eight-thirty was a matter of having stepped out for a cigarette and exchanged a word with the dining steward, no more. What he was hiding, as far as the case ran, was nothing of substance — his professional antagonism toward Pemberton over the Marsh attribution was a matter of documented record, his contempt for the man was honest, and he had genuinely had no occasion to go near the port decanter. He was the satisfying suspect — motive on paper, placement in the corridor, declared opponent of the deceased — who could not, finally, account for the mechanism. *He produced the dinner-receipt and the cloakroom ticket with a small, lawyerly precision, and when asked about the corridor at eight-thirty he gave the cigarette and the steward without elaboration, and his eyes did not lift from the detective's pen while he spoke.*

### Hugh Emberly (member, club essayist)

He spoke in the cultivated, faintly affected English of a man who had published three slim volumes of essays in the twenties and had been dining at the Aldgate three nights a week ever since. His vocabulary was mid-shelf literary — *a most distinguished mind*, *the Marsh estate*, *the late Pemberton* — and his sentences were unhurried and well-formed. Under pressure his composure did not break; he had nothing to hide and gave what he had seen without performance. His alibi was the smoking room from eight until nine-thirty, witnessed by Norris and Dent; what he had was a clear recollection of seeing Mrs Vail in the corridor at eight-twenty with a glass in her hand, which he had not thought significant at the time. *He named the glass without prompting and described its shape, and his hand traced the bowl of it once in the air as he spoke.*

### Colonel Norris (member, retired military, port pourer)

He spoke in clipped, complete sentences, in the order he had been asked, and answered no question he had not been asked. His vocabulary was the small ceremonial language of the club he had loved for forty years — *the eight o'clock*, *the table*, *the decanter*, *the steward*. Under pressure he stood straighter, not less straight; he had decanted the port himself from the cellar bottle, as he had for twenty years, and had carried the tray to the reading room as he always did at eight. He had set the tray down on the side table. He had then returned to the smoking room. His alibi was the smoking room; what he was hiding was nothing — the matter of the tray having been left unattended in the reading room from eight until Pemberton came down at nine-thirty was a matter of standing club practice, and it had not occurred to him as a vulnerability. *He gave the bottle, the table, the hour and the steward in the order he had walked it, and when asked who else had been in the reading room corridor between eight and nine he said only that he had not looked, and he did not look while he said it.*

### Lillian March (publisher's representative)

She spoke in the practiced, faintly editorial cadence of a woman who had read manuscripts for a living for fifteen years and had learned to speak as carefully as she edited. Her sentences were complete, well-constructed, and delivered with the small deliberate pauses of someone choosing words. Under pressure she did not over-explain; she became more precise. Her alibi was the dining room with Carey's party until shortly after nine, after which she had taken the late train to Hampstead. What she was hiding was a private exchange with Pemberton on Tuesday afternoon in which the matter of the Marsh attribution had been discussed — the publisher had, in fact, been informed of the impending correction, and the firm's response had been measured. *She named the Tuesday meeting only when asked and gave its substance in three sentences and did not offer the fourth, and her hand rested flat on the publisher's letter the whole while she spoke.*

### Percival Dent (publisher, gentleman of the firm)

He spoke in the slow, mannered baritone of a publisher of the old school, who had inherited the firm from his father and who believed, in a quiet way, that a book was the only honest measure of a life. His vocabulary was older than him — *the dear chap*, *the bound proof*, *the dedication leaf*, *one supposes* — and his sentences finished cleanly. Under pressure he did not tighten; he went genuinely sorrowful, and the small motion of his pipe-bowl against his palm became more frequent. His alibi was the smoking room from seven until ten, witnessed by half the membership; what he was hiding was an honest grief at Pemberton's death and a private uncertainty about the Marsh attribution that he had not, until this evening, allowed himself to articulate. *He said the late Mr Pemberton had been a man of unusual exactness, and his pipe-bowl turned against his palm twice before he said the next sentence, which was that the attribution had always struck him, on quiet reflection, as something not quite right.*

### Dr Pryce (member, club physician, examined the body)

He spoke in the precise, unembellished register of a Harley Street physician who had been brought down from the smoking room at ten fifteen and asked to confirm what was already obvious. His vocabulary was clinical — *cardiac arrest*, *the patient's history*, *approximately ninety minutes*, *consistent with* — and he used it sparingly. Under pressure he did not soften; he became more exact. His alibi was the smoking room and Norris's testimony; what he had was the time-of-death estimate that the case would later turn on. *He gave ninety minutes and named the cardiac history and named the patient's irregularities in the order he had recorded them, and he did not speculate beyond what the body had told him.*

## Witnesses

### Wills the porter (top NPC, full voice arc — the case's pivotal witness)

He spoke in the slow, courteous register of a club servant who had been at the Aldgate door since 1908, who had stopped trying to be remembered by guests two decades ago, and who remembered every face that had ever crossed his threshold. His vocabulary was the small ceremonial language of his post — *the book*, *the lodge*, *the key*, *the gentleman*, *the lady* — and his sentences were unhurried, exact, and offered without colour. He did not interpret; he gave fact. Under pressure he did not change; pressure was a thing other men felt. His tell, when there was one, was that he stopped to think before answering a question he had not been asked before — and there were very few of those. His alibi was the porter's lodge from seven until eleven; what he had was the sign-in book, the key log, the memory of a face he had seen at the decanter for seven minutes between eight-fifteen and eight-twenty-two, and a precise account of why he had not, at the time, looked across to the side table. He had said nothing of the seven minutes because no one had asked him in the right way: the question had to specify the matter of the decanter, the matter of his having stepped from the lodge to take a member's hat, the matter of the seven minutes. *He said he had taken Mr Dent's hat at the eight-fifteen and remembered the eight-twenty-two because the bell had sounded, and when asked who had been at the side table in the reading room during those seven minutes he said the lady in the black gloves and named her without embellishment, and the sign-in book in his lap remained open at the eight-five entry the whole while.*

### Archivist Kemp (manuscript archive)

He spoke in the dry, faintly bibliographic register of a club archivist who had cataloged the Marsh correspondence in 1931 and had not been consulted since. His vocabulary was archival — *the catalogue number*, *the folder*, *the holograph*, *the typescript* — and he answered questions about manuscript provenance with the slow exactness of a man for whom the case was an interruption to a quiet life among papers. He had no tell because he had nothing to hide; what he had was the Marsh correspondence file and the unrecorded fact that two letters from 1921, in a woman's hand, had been delivered to Pemberton's executorship in 1930 and had not since been returned. *He said the folder had been requested by Mr Pemberton in October and had not been returned to the archive, and he opened the catalogue at the entry and turned it for her to read.*

### The club secretary

She spoke in the brisk, efficient register of a woman who had run the Aldgate's administrative life for twelve years and considered the murder a regrettable interruption to membership renewals. Her sentences were short, factual, and offered without warmth. She had no tell. Her alibi was the secretary's office on the first floor until eight-forty-five; what she had was the membership roll, the records of correspondence between Pemberton's office and the club, and a clear recollection of Mrs Vail's signing in at eight-five. *She named the eight-five entry without consulting the book and named the eight-forty exit the same way, and when asked whether Mrs Vail had been alone she said only that no other lady had signed in after.*

### The dining steward

He spoke in the easy, professional register of a club steward of the old school, his attention divided between the question and the dining-room door. His sentences were complete, polite, and offered without elaboration. He had no tell of consequence. His alibi was the dining room from six-thirty until ten; what he had was a clear placement of Carey near the reading-room corridor at half-past eight, the cigarette Carey had stepped out to smoke, and the word they had exchanged about the weather. *He named the cigarette and the corridor and the half-hour and the word about the weather, and his eyes returned to the dining-room door between sentences, and there was nothing more to give.*

### The biographer Holm

He spoke in the eager, slightly under-rested register of a researcher who had been working on the Marsh biography for two years and had been brought to the club at the detective's request. His sentences were long, parenthetical, and full of small detours through bibliographic detail. His vocabulary was scholarly — *the holograph correspondence*, *the textual evidence*, *the variant readings* — and he used it with care. He had no tell because he had nothing to hide; what he had was the request he had filed in October for the 1918–1922 correspondence, the delays he had encountered, and his quiet conviction, supported now by archival access, that the hand in the Marsh letters from 1918 onward did not match the hand in the manuscripts of 1919. *He produced the letter requesting the correspondence and the typescript of his chapter five, and he laid them side by side on the smoking-room table and named the letters in date order and waited.*
