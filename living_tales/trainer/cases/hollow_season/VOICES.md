# Hollow Season — Voices

## Narrative voice

The case is told in third-person past tense, in the manner of a recovered country-house record. The detective is the camera; the house acts upon her — its slanting summer light, its shut rooms, its bells ringing for meals that have not been sat down to — and she acts upon it, and the prose registers both without commentary. There are no thoughts, no feelings, no wonderings. Only what was seen, what was heard, what was lifted from a wastepaper basket, and what was inferred from those things in the flat, observational cadence of an Obra Dinn cartouche. Sentences are short. Verbs are concrete. A sensory anchor — the rooks settling in the limes, the dust motes hanging in a south window, the brass of a service bell, the smell of cut grass coming in off the lawn — grounds every scene before any human enters it.

The setting is Ashworth Park, a country house in the West Riding, August 1907. The harvest is in but the season has gone hollow with it; the days are still long but a coolness has come into the mornings and the rooms east of the chimneystack stay cold until nine. Twelve staff maintain a house that earns enough for nine. Speech, when it appears, arrives reported and clipped — *he said the front door had been locked at eleven and the side door at quarter past, and that was the order in which he had locked them every Saturday for thirty-one years, and he gave the times in the order he had given them*. NPCs are never editorialised; their disquiet is shown by what their hands did, where their eyes failed to go, which doorframe they touched as they entered. The voice trusts the reader to assemble the case from observed surfaces, exactly as the detective must. Above all, the voice registers what Edwardian protocol calls for and what was done instead — a servant addressed by a Christian name, a letter burned that ought to have been answered, a bell not rung that ought to have been.

## Suspects

### Edmund Carrow — nephew, heir, the killer

He spoke in the careful, unhurried English of a man who had been listening to other people speak in the house since he was twelve and had learnt early what register the room expected. His sentences were complete and finished cleanly; he did not over-explain. Under pressure he became more measured rather than less — the pauses lengthened by a beat, the diction tightened by a degree, the hand that touched the doorframe when he entered the library stayed at his side instead. His voice did not rise. His eyes did not slide. The tells he produced were small and almost domestic — the eleven minutes he stood before his mother's portrait on the Saturday before dinner; the half-second pause when the audit was mentioned at breakfast; the way he addressed Mrs Lyle by her surname when he had grown up calling her by her Christian one. None of these were evidence. Together they were a portrait of a man who could not afford to lose the house. *He gave the detective an account of his Saturday evening — dinner, withdrawal, bed by eleven — and he gave it in the order in which it had happened, and when the chemist's receipt was placed in front of him he did not pale, he did not tighten, he simply waited a half-second longer than he had waited for any of the questions before, and then he said he could not account for it.* His alibi was the village rooms and a bedside lamp; what he was hiding was the brandy, the window, the receipt, and the geography of the house in his own chest.

### Hector Finch — estate manager, the red herring

He spoke in the careful, slightly aggrieved English of a man who had worked eleven years for a family he was not part of and had learnt to make his voice useful and small. His sentences began with concessive clauses — *insofar as the accounts can be said to show, by my own reckoning, with the proper allowance for the timber prices —* and his hands moved as he spoke, finding the corner of a ledger, the edge of a desk, the wire of a pince-nez. Under pressure he over-explained, producing dates and entries that had not been asked for, and the over-explanation was a long-rehearsed habit. The audit was on Monday. He knew what it would find. He had not killed Sir Geoffrey. He had spent eleven years not being noticed and the not-being-noticed had narrowed him into the shape of a guilty man whether he was one or not. *He told the detective the timber prices for 1904 and 1905 and 1906 without being asked, and he told them in falling order, and the silence after the last figure was longer than any pause he had taken between the figures.* His alibi was the gatehouse lodge and a desk lamp; what he was hiding was three years of skimming the timber accounts and a debt he had come to this posting carrying.

### Mrs Lyle — housekeeper, the bridge

She spoke in the warm, measured English of a woman who had been at Ashworth Park since she was nineteen and had outlived two ladies of the house. Her sentences were complete and rounded; she used the proper form for everyone and did not abbreviate. Under pressure she did not change. Her usefulness, when it came, was not produced by pressure at all — it had been sitting in a green sack in the back scullery since Sunday morning, when she had emptied the upstairs wastepaper baskets at the village rooms on her weekly visit and not yet got round to sorting the paper for reuse. *She said she had emptied Master Edmund's basket on the Sunday morning, as she had done every Sunday morning since he had taken the village rooms, and that she had not yet sorted the paper, and when the detective asked if the receipt might still be in the sack she said it might and that she would fetch the sack and she fetched it.* Her alibi was the housekeeping rounds and the housekeeper's room. She was hiding nothing — except the small, careful affection for Edmund that made the bringing of the sack take her a quarter of an hour longer than it ought to have done.

### Mr Croft — solicitor's assistant

He spoke quickly and at length, in the careful, slightly over-formal English of a young man whose position depended on being articulate. His sentences began with subordinate clauses — *to the extent that the matter is within my knowledge, on the understanding that I speak only for myself —* and his hands moved as he spoke, finding the cuff of a sleeve, the brim of a hat, the catch of a postbox he had walked past twice on the Friday evening before he posted the note. Under pressure he over-explained the procedural side and went quiet on the personal one. The debt to Edmund he did not mention until the third time it was put to him. *He gave the detective the post-office record for the Friday evening collection without being asked and named the time of his own walk past the box and did not mention, until the question came a third time, that he had walked past the box because he had been carrying a note that he had not at first been sure he was going to post.* His alibi was the office and the village postbox; what he was hiding was the small, foolish loyalty that had sent the note in the end.

### Mrs Penrose — sister-in-law, the lady of the house

She spoke in the practised, slightly distant English of a woman who had run a house with twelve staff for nineteen years and had learnt to put words in their place. Her sentences were short and exact; she gave the order of the Saturday dinner and the time of the withdrawal as though reading from a household book. The tell was the exactness — she remembered the hour because she had remembered it on purpose. Under pressure she did not soften. Her cooperation was the cooperation of someone who had decided in advance what she would say and would say no more. *She said the dinner had been laid for eight at half past seven and the withdrawal had been at half past nine and that she had retired to her own sitting room at a quarter past ten, and she gave the times in the order in which they had occurred and offered no further detail about any of the hours between them.* Her alibi was her own sitting room and the ladies' maid's later lamp on the back stair; what she was hiding was that she had known, by the Friday evening, what Geoffrey meant to do with the new will, and that she had not warned Edmund.

## Witnesses

### Hadley — butler

He spoke in the slow, even cadence of a man who had been a butler at Ashworth Park for thirty-one years and considered the keys at his belt the proper authority for any question about the house. His sentences were short, official, and resistant to embellishment. He gave times in the order he had walked them. Under pressure he became more formal rather than less. He kept his white cotton gloves on for the duration of the interview. *He said he had locked the front door at eleven and the side door at quarter past on Saturday evening, as he had done every Saturday evening for thirty-one years, and that the french windows of the library had not been on his round because Sir Geoffrey kept them himself, and he gave each piece of information once and did not repeat it.* His alibi was the duty log and the pantry key on his belt. He was hiding nothing.

### the under-gardener

He spoke in fragments and short sentences, in the manner of a man who had been in the garden since six and was not used to being asked at length about what he had seen. His sentences were trimmed of pronouns and ran on slightly when he tried to fill them out. The tell was simply his discomfort at being indoors. *He said he had come in at six on the Sunday morning, as he did most mornings in August, to draw the canes before the day got into the south wall, and that he had seen the french windows of the library standing open then, and that the curtain on the left side had been on the outside of the frame, which he had not seen before.* His alibi was the bean canes and the south wall. He was hiding nothing.

### the ladies' maid

She spoke softly, in the careful, slightly nervous English of a woman who had been in service for fourteen years and did not enjoy being the subject of a sentence. Her sentences were short and complete and tended to apologise at the end. The tell was the apology. *She said she had lit the lamp on the back stair at half past ten on Saturday for Master Edmund's return, as Mrs Penrose had asked her to, and that the lamp was still alight when she went up at half past eleven, and that no one had come up the back stair in the interval, but that she might be wrong about that and was very sorry if she was.* Her alibi was the lamp and the back stair. She was hiding nothing.

### the village chemist

He spoke in the dry, precise English of a man who had kept a chemist's shop on the village high street for twenty-six years and made it his business to remember every bottle he sold. His sentences were short, official, and entirely accurate. He produced his counter book when asked and not before. *He said he had sold one bottle of the preparation on the Friday afternoon, between four and five, and that he had entered the sale in the counter book without the buyer's name, as he was permitted to do with the preparation in the smaller doses, and that the buyer had not been a man he was prepared to identify until the receipt was placed in front of him.* His alibi was the shop and the counter book. He was hiding nothing.

### Mr Aldhurst — solicitor

He spoke in the careful, institutional English of a country solicitor who had drafted documents for three generations of the family and had no intention of saying anything that would commit him beyond the document. His sentences were complete, polite, and slightly recursive — *to the extent that I am in a position to say, and with appropriate caution as to what may yet be witnessed —* and his vocabulary belonged to the deed box more than to the dinner table. Under pressure he became more legalistic. *He said the new will had been drafted on the Thursday morning and signed by Sir Geoffrey in his own hand on the Thursday afternoon, and that a second witness had been required and had not yet been secured by the Sunday morning, and that the document as it stood was therefore inadmissible without further action, and he gave each clause in the order in which it appeared in the document.* His alibi was the Saturday dinner and the guest room. He was hiding nothing — except the small, professional embarrassment of having drafted a will that had not survived the weekend.
