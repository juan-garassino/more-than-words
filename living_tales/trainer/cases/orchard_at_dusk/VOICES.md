# Orchard at Dusk — Voices

## Narrative voice

The case is told in third-person past tense, in the manner of a recovered parish record. The detective is the camera; the orchard acts upon her — the apples dropping into long grass, the cider press turning in the shed below the slope, the late September light flattening between the bare west branches — and she acts upon the orchard, and the prose registers both without commentary. There are no thoughts, no feelings, no wonderings. Only what was seen, what was heard, what was lifted from a parish register, and what was inferred from those things in the flat, methodical cadence of an Obra Dinn cartouche. Sentences are short. Verbs are concrete. A sensory anchor — the smell of cut grass and bruised apple, the lowing of the rooks in the limes, the damp at the foot of the boundary wall — grounds every scene before any human enters it.

The setting is the village of Thornfield, in the Mendip country south of Bristol, in the third week of September 1903. The harvest is in; the cider apples are still coming off the trees. The days are still warm at noon but the dusk comes early and the long grass goes black at the orchard's edge by half past seven. The world is rural, Edwardian, churchgoing — speech is shaped by the parish register and the pulpit and the tap-room of the Hawk and Shoe, and turns of phrase carry the cadence of West Country labouring English without lapsing into dialect caricature. Speech, when it appears, arrives reported and clipped — *he said the gate had been moved on Friday and that he could not say when on Friday, and he said it the way a man says the time he heard the church clock strike*. NPCs are never editorialised; their concealment is shown by what their hands did with their hats, by which way they looked when the orchard was named, by the prayer book closed mid-sentence on the vestry table. The voice trusts the reader to assemble the case from observed surfaces, exactly as the detective must.

## Suspects

### Thomas Crale — farmer, the killer

He spoke in the slow, even cadence of a West Country farmer who had stood at his own gate for thirty-five years and was used to being asked questions about cattle, weather, and the harvest. His sentences were short and complete; he did not over-explain. Under pressure he became more deliberate, not less — the pauses lengthened by a beat, the diction tightened to a degree that suggested he had thought the answer through before being asked, and the right hand that customarily rested on the gate stayed at his side. His voice did not rise. The tells he gave were small and pastoral — the apple turned slowly between thumb and finger as he spoke of the boundary, the half-second he took before he named the hour of supper, the way he addressed the constable by his Christian name when the inquest was first mentioned and by his rank thereafter. None of these were evidence. Together they were the portrait of a man who had spent nineteen years rehearsing for the day a question would come. *He told the detective his account of the Friday — the upper field at noon, the press at three, the gate at four for repairs, the kitchen for supper at seven — and he gave it in the order in which it had happened, and when the 1884 receipt was set on the kitchen table he did not pale and did not tighten; he simply put down the apple and waited a half-second longer than he had waited for any question before, and then he said he could not account for it.* His alibi was the supper at seven and Hob Tilling at the table opposite; what he was hiding was the gate at four, the lane at half past, and nineteen years of silence with a man dead five years already.

### Amos Webb — neighbour, the red herring

He spoke in the loud, direct English of a man whose voice had carried across a yard since he was twelve and whose temper had been general knowledge in Thornfield since he was twenty. His sentences were short, declarative, and frequently angry, and he repeated himself when he felt he had not been believed the first time. Under pressure he did not close — he opened, louder. *He has had me down seven acres since the drainage went in, and the survey is a lie, and any man with eyes can read the lie, and the man who wrote it is dead now and I'd say the same if he stood here.* He had no concealment to manage; his anger was the surface of his anger and there was no second surface beneath it. The tells he produced were the tells of a man who had been wronged and knew it — the fist on the inn table on Thursday evening, the hand that went to the back of the neck when the inquest verdict was mentioned, the long silence at the orchard fence after the argument while he looked not at the trees but at the Crale farmhouse, because something about Crale he had sensed the edges of for nineteen years without ever bringing it forward into a sentence. *He named the survey a lie three times in the first interview and named Crale a thief once, and when the detective asked whether he had any further reason to think it he stopped and looked at the floor for nine seconds and said only that there was a thing he had never been able to put words to.* His alibi was his own farm and his wife and Martha Ainsworth at the kitchen table from four to seven on Friday; what he was hiding was nothing — except the shape of a suspicion he had carried for nineteen years without ever quite seeing.

### Hob Tilling — farmhand, the unwitting alibi

He spoke in the soft, slightly hurried English of a Mendip-country boy of twenty-three who had been three seasons on Crale Farm and had never been the principal subject of an interview in his life. His sentences were short and complete and tended to end with a small upward inflection, as though he were checking whether what he had said had been right. He used the proper form for Crale and for the constable and the detective and would not call any of them by anything else. Under pressure he did not change — he simply went slower and the cap in his hand turned a full revolution between sentences. The tells he gave were the tells of a young man telling the truth and watching the truth become useful in a way he did not yet understand. *He said he had taken his supper with Mr Crale at the kitchen table at seven on Friday evening, as he had taken supper there every Friday since Lady Day, and that they had sat for the better part of an hour, and when the detective asked where he had been before supper he said he had been in the upper field cutting the last of the hay until half past five, and then at the press until six, and that he had not seen Mr Crale between two o'clock and seven, and he said it the way a boy says the times the church clock had struck because the church clock was the only clock he had been near all afternoon.* His alibi was Crale's, and Crale's was his, but only for supper; what he was hiding was nothing. The afternoon gap was not a thing he had ever thought to lie about.

## Witnesses

### Reverend Caddick — vicar of St Bartholomew's

He spoke in the careful, measured English of a country parson of twenty-six years' standing, who had been at the side of a great many deathbeds and had married a great many of the parish, and was accustomed to taking his time before he gave a name. His sentences were complete and rounded and frequently began with a small concession — *as far as my recollection holds*, *though I would not swear to the year*. Under pressure he did not change but he slowed. The tell, when it came, was the prayer book closed on the vestry table mid-sentence, the right hand placed flat over the front board, the eyes lifted to the south window before the answer was given. He had buried Alderton in 1898 and he had married Eliza Crale in 1882 and he remembered the autumn of 1884 because he had been the one who had not asked Crale a question he ought to have asked. *He said he had known Mr Alderton from his arrival in the parish in 1881 to his burial in 1898, and that there had been a matter in the autumn of 1884 which had been a matter between Mr Alderton and Mr Crale and not a matter for the church, and that he could not now recall what shape the matter had taken, but he had been aware at the time that money had moved which would not have stood the asking, and he said it with his hand flat on the prayer book and did not say more without further question.* His alibi was the vestry and the parish register; what he was hiding was the long silence he had kept since 1884, which was not the silence of a guilty man but the silence of a churchman who had decided once, nineteen years ago, that the matter was not his to bring forward.

### Constable Briggs — village constable

He spoke as though answering a superior officer, in clipped, complete sentences that began with *sir* or *madam* and ended squarely. His vocabulary was procedural by habit — *the inquest*, *the verdict*, *the body*, *the deposition* — and he did not embellish. Under pressure he stood straighter, not less straight. The tell was that the inquest book in his hand stayed closed when he spoke of the verdict and opened when he spoke of the position of the body, and that he himself had noticed, on the Saturday morning, that the long grass along the boundary path had been combed flat where a man's boots had passed. *He said the coroner had returned a verdict of accidental death in accordance with the medical evidence, and that the body had lain in the long grass two yards from the drainage stone, and that he had seen no other footprints in the grass besides those of the deceased, and that he had not reported the absence of other footprints because the absence of footprints had not been a thing the inquest had asked after.* His alibi was the inquest, the parish station, and the duty log. He was hiding nothing.

### Ned Sowerby — innkeeper at the Hawk and Shoe

He spoke in the easy, practiced English of a village publican of fourteen years' standing, who had taken his time learning his customers' names and his time forgetting their quarrels. His sentences were short and observational and tended to begin with *aye* and end with *and that was the end of it*. Under pressure he did not soften and did not stiffen; he simply slowed. The tell was the cloth in his hand, which kept moving across the bar the entire time he spoke. He had heard the Thursday-evening confrontation between Webb and Oates from behind the bar and remembered who had paid for what before, during, and after. *He said Mr Webb had come in at half past seven with a private measurement on a sheet of paper and had set the paper before Mr Oates, and that Mr Webb had spoken loud enough that the snug had emptied of conversation for some four or five minutes, and that Mr Oates had spoken quietly throughout, and that Mr Oates had agreed before he left to go to Bristol on Monday and to file a correction, and that he had said it without looking up from the table, and he said it with the cloth still moving across the bar.* His alibi was the bar of the Hawk and Shoe and the till book. He was hiding nothing.

### Eliza Crale — Thomas Crale's wife

She spoke quietly, in the soft consonants of a Mendip-country woman of fifty-two who had been twenty-nine years in the Crale farmhouse and had learnt early which questions her husband answered and which she did not. Her sentences were short and complete and finished without embellishment. Under pressure she did not flinch; she went still, and the cloth she was folding on the kitchen table stopped moving in her hands. The tell, when it came, was the kettle that came off the hob unprompted, the cup that was set down on the deal table without offering, the moment when she did not say her husband's Christian name although she had said it twice in the sentence before. *She said the supper of Friday evening had been laid for seven o'clock as it had been every Friday since the boy Tilling had been put on, and that her husband had been at the table when she set the bread down, and that she could not say what he had done with the afternoon because she had been at the cider press with the books from three until half past six, and she said it without looking up from the cloth.* Her alibi was the cider press ledger and the books. What she was hiding was the same thing her husband was hiding, but without ever having spoken of it to him.

### Martha Ainsworth — Amos Webb's sister-in-law

She spoke in the short, exact English of a working woman of thirty-seven who had kept the Webb farmhouse since her sister's marriage and was not used to being the subject of a question. Her sentences were brief and given without elaboration. Under pressure she did not soften; she gave only what she had seen, and only the first time it was asked. The tell was that she gave the time of the sighting precisely — by the kitchen clock — without being asked to be precise. *She said she had been at the kitchen window of Webb Farm at four o'clock on the Friday afternoon, and had seen Mr Crale on the orchard lane heading toward the field gate, and that he had been alone, and that she had not seen him return, and that the kitchen clock had been at four because she had wound it that morning and it had not yet been wrong, and she said it once.* Her alibi was the kitchen window and her own sister at the kitchen table. She was hiding nothing.
